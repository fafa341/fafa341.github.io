The Factory That Prices Products Out of Thin Air

A Chilean stainless steel manufacturer. Every product different. One pricing method for all of them.

Ingeniería en Aceros (Dulox) is a Chilean stainless steel manufacturer. They make custom products for restaurants, industrial kitchens, hotels, and construction sites. Industrial countertops. Custom shelves. Cylindrical trash bins with mirror-polish finish. Campanas for restaurant kitchens. Every product is different. Different dimensions, different processes, different complexity.

For years, their pricing method has been: cost of materials × a factor. That factor is meant to absorb all factory overhead — operators, machines, time, consumables — spread uniformly across everything. It's simple, it's fast, and it's been good enough to keep the company profitable.

They've been profitable for years. So the factor works — on average. The factory makes money overall. But they have no idea which products make money and which ones don't. They don't know what to prioritize. They can't quote competitively because they don't know their real costs. And when the salesperson quotes a complex product too low, nobody catches it until the order is halfway through production.

The problem isn't that the factor is wrong. The problem is that it treats a simple shelf the same as a complex cylindrical trash bin with interior welds and a mirror-polish finish. They are not the same product. Not even close. The factor collapses all of that variation into a single number — and in doing so, it destroys the information you need to make good decisions.

Section 02
02

The Real Problem Is Decomposition

Before you can price something, you have to be able to break it into its cost primitives.

For months, I thought this was a pricing problem. Fix the formula, update the multiplier, refine the factor. But that misses it entirely. This isn't a pricing problem. It's a decomposition problem.

Before you can price something, you have to be able to decompose it — break it into its cost primitives. For a stainless steel product, those primitives are: materials, direct labor per process, machine time, consumables. The reason the factor has worked is that nobody had done this decomposition. It's hard. It requires sitting with the production manager and actually mapping out what happens to a product from raw steel sheet to finished piece.

The real challenge: every product is custom, so you can't have a fixed price list. You need a model that generalizes — that can take any new product and estimate its cost without having measured it before.

The structural decision

Classify products into profiles (what processes they go through) and complexity tiers (how hard each process is for this specific product). If you know both, you can compute cost. If you can generalize both to new products, you have a pricing engine.

Section 03
03

What a Product Is Determines What It Costs

The first task: classify every SKU by its manufacturing identity, not its commercial category.

Step one was going through all 1,300+ SKUs and classifying each one into a perfil_proceso — a process profile. A process profile answers a specific question: what sequence of manufacturing operations does this product go through?

Two products that look nothing alike can share a profile if they go through the same factory operations. A cylindrical trash bin and a custom pot both go through rolling (cilindrado), welding, and polishing — their process profiles are identical. A flat shelf and a counter panel share cutting, bending, and welding. The grouping is by manufacturing identity, not by shape or appearance.

It would have been easy to make dozens of micro-categories. We resisted. The goal was the smallest number of profiles that still captures real cost variation. Too many profiles and the model becomes unmaintainable. Too few and it loses resolution.
Section 04
04

Four Numbers That Explain Complexity

The insight: instead of asking "how complex is this product," ask what physical parameters drive manufacturing difficulty.

Knowing the process profile tells you which operations a product goes through. But it doesn't tell you how hard each operation is for this specific product. A cylindrical bin that's 30 cm wide is not the same as one that's 120 cm wide. Both go through the same profile. Their costs are very different.

We landed on four universal drivers, each scored 1 to 3. Every product gets scored on all four. The scores feed each process's complexity calculation.

G
Geometry — Surface Area
The amount of steel the product consumes. Larger products require more cutting, more welding, more polishing, more material. Surface area is the most direct proxy for scale.
scales with all processes
D
Density — Material Thickness
A 1mm product is not the same as a 5mm product. Thicker steel is harder to cut, harder to bend, requires more powerful machines, demands more skilled operators. It also changes the consumables profile.
cutting, bending, welding
C
Components — Part Count
A product with 4 components versus 12 components is fundamentally different in assembly complexity. More components means more joints, more welding, more fit-up, more chances for dimensional error.
welding, assembly, QC
X
Expert Variables — Complexity Flags
Everything the operators know intuitively that doesn't fit into G, D, or C. Interior welds. Mirror polish. Logo etching. Refrigeration integration. Binary flags that formalize 30 years of intuition.
process-specific flags
Each process uses a subset of the four drivers. Welding uses C and X. Polishing uses G and X. Bending uses G, D, and C. Each combination gives a score. That score maps to a complexity tier: C1 (standard), C2 (elevated), C3 (expert). Each tier has calibrated time estimates derived from real production measurements.

Eleven Processes, Three Tiers Each

Make the model concrete. A product's cost is the sum of its active cells.

There are eleven manufacturing processes that cover everything Dulox does. Each process, for each product, gets assigned C1, C2, or C3. Each tier has a calibrated time estimate (in minutes), an operator count, and a consumable package. These came from direct production measurement — chronometer sessions with the production manager, watching each operation on real anchor products.

The cost per process equals time × operator rate, plus machine depreciation, plus consumables. The total product cost is the sum across all active processes. You can't measure every product — there are 1,300 SKUs. So you calibrate on seven anchor products: the most common, covering the widest range of processes and complexity. Everything else extrapolates from the nearest anchor, with dimensions scaling the time estimates proportionally.

Section 06
06

The Knowledge Problem

The hardest part of the project had nothing to do with data or models.

The production manager at Dulox — Hernán — has worked there for over thirty years. He knows, intuitively, how long every process takes. He knows which products are going to be trouble before they're even started. He knows which operators can handle which complexity levels. He's never been wrong in a way that cost the company its shirt.

This knowledge is real. It's accurate. And it is completely informal. It exists only in his head. There's no documentation. No training manual. No spreadsheet. Just thirty years of pattern recognition, embedded in one person.

The challenge: how do you extract, formalize, and make consistent something that someone has never had to articulate? You can't just interview him once. The knowledge is contextual, process-specific, and changes as machines change, operators learn, and new product types appear.

The first sessions were hard. Hernán would say things like "that takes about 40 minutes" and when you asked "for what size piece?" he'd think for a second and say "well, it depends." The job was to make the it depends explicit.

Formalization changes things. When you ask an expert to articulate their knowledge, they often realize they don't know it as precisely as they thought. The process of articulation creates new clarity — and reveals new uncertainty. This is uncomfortable. The expert feels exposed. The interviewer has to be careful not to undermine the trust that makes the extraction possible.

Intercepting the Existing Workflow

The failure mode of most digitization projects: you ask people to do new things. Don't.

Resistance is the default outcome when you ask people who've done something one way for fifteen years to do it differently. New forms, new software, new habits. Every new step is friction. Friction becomes abandonment. I've seen it enough times to not try it.

The insight was simpler: don't change the workflow. Find the existing artifact that already captures the information you need, and build the system around that artifact.

What already exists: when a production order arrives, Hernán draws a sketch. He's always drawn sketches. The sketch goes to each operator with handwritten annotations. It's his primary communication tool. It contains everything — dimensions, material specs, process indications, complexity flags, special notes for operators. Everything we need.

Section 08
08

Augmentation, Not Replacement

The easy story is "we used AI to price products." That misses the point entirely.

The vision model is not the innovation. The classification model is not the innovation. The innovation is the epistemological work: making the implicit explicit, structuring what was intuitive, building a system that can hold and communicate expert knowledge consistently across time and personnel.

Hernán is not being replaced. He is being equipped with a mirror — a system that reflects his own knowledge back to him in a form that can be shared, challenged, updated, and improved. When the model is wrong (and it will be, especially early), Hernán corrects it. The correction teaches the model. The model becomes more accurate. Hernán becomes more consistent because he has to articulate the exception.

This is a feedback loop between human expertise and machine structure. Neither works without the other.

The deeper claim

The most valuable application of AI in manufacturing is not automation of tasks. It's the formalization of expertise. The 30-year veteran who retires takes his knowledge with him. If you've built the system, the knowledge stays.

This is hard. It takes longer than you expect. The expert has to trust you. The organization has to commit to maintaining the model. The data quality depends on human diligence. There is no version of this that runs itself.

But when it works, something unusual happens: a company that has operated on intuition for thirty years suddenly has a language for discussing its own costs. Salespeople can explain a quote. The production manager can flag a product as underpriced before it's manufactured. Junior staff can onboard to the pricing system in weeks instead of years.

The factory didn't change. The machines are the same. The operators are the same. Hernán still draws his sketches every morning. What changed is that now, when the sketch is done, the number is right.
