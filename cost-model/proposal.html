S.01 · The Problem

The Factory That Prices Products Out of Thin Air

A Chilean stainless steel manufacturer. Every product different. One pricing method for all of them.

Ingeniería en Aceros makes custom stainless steel products for restaurants, industrial kitchens, hotels, and construction sites across Chile. Everything is custom — different dimensions, different finishes, different manufacturing processes for every single order. A restaurant campana, a cylindrical trash bin with a pedal mechanism, an industrial counter with built-in refrigeration. They are not the same product. Not even close.

For thirty years, the pricing method has been: cost of materials × a factor. That factor is supposed to absorb all factory overhead — operators, machines, time, consumables — spread uniformly across everything.

S.02 · The Insight

The Real Problem Is Decomposition

This isn't a pricing problem. It's a decomposition problem.

Before you can price something, you have to decompose it — break it into its cost primitives. For a stainless steel product: materials, direct labor per process, machine time, consumables. The reason the factor has worked is that nobody had done this decomposition. It's hard. It requires sitting with the production manager and actually mapping out what happens to a product from raw steel sheet to finished piece.

The real challenge: every product is custom, so you can't have a fixed price list. You need a model that generalizes — that can take any new product and estimate cost without having measured it before.

The structural decision

Classify products into profiles (what processes they go through) and complexity tiers (how hard each process is for this specific product). If you know both, you can compute cost. If you can generalize both to new products, you have a pricing engine.

S.04 · Four Complexity Drivers
Four numbers that explain difficulty
instead of asking "how complex?" — ask what physical parameters drive difficulty
04

Once you know the profile (which processes), you still need to know the complexity — how hard each process is for this specific product. The insight: instead of asking "how complex is this product overall," ask what are the physical parameters that drive difficulty in manufacturing? We landed on four universal drivers, each scored 1–5.

G
Geometry
surface_area(mm²) → scale
The amount of steel the product consumes. Larger products require more cutting, more welding, more polishing, more material. Surface area is the most direct proxy for production time.
D
Density
thickness(mm) → difficulty
A 1mm product is not the same as a 5mm product. Thicker steel is harder to cut, harder to bend, requires more powerful machines, demands more skilled operators. It also changes the consumable profile — welding wire, argon, blade wear.
C
Components
part_count → assembly complexity
A product with 4 components versus 12 is fundamentally different. More components means more joints, more welding, more fit-up, more chances for dimensional error. Every join is a decision point for the operator.
X
Expert Variables
flags[] → intuition formalized
Everything operators know intuitively that doesn't fit neatly into geometry, density, or count. Interior welds. Mirror polish. Logo etching. Refrigeration systems. These are binary flags that add points to the system — the formalization of 30 years of "it depends."
Each process uses a subset of the four drivers. Welding uses C and X. Polishing uses G and X. Bending uses G, D, and C. Each combination gives a score. That score maps to a complexity tier: C1 through C5. Each tier has calibrated time estimates derived from real production measurements.

S.06 · The Knowledge Problem

It Lives in One Person's Head

The production manager has been working at Dulox for over 30 years. The real challenge wasn't building the model. It was extracting the knowledge to feed it.

"Hernán knows, intuitively, how long every process takes. He knows which products are going to be trouble before they're even started. He knows which operators can handle which complexity. This knowledge is real, it's accurate, and it is completely informal."

You can't just interview him once. The knowledge is contextual, process-specific, and changes as machines change, operators learn, and new product types appear. The first sessions were hard. He would say "that takes about 40 minutes" and when you asked "for what size piece?" he'd think for a second and say "well, it depends." The job was to make the "it depends" explicit.

We ran calibration sessions with a chronometer — timing actual production of anchor products that cover the widest range of processes and complexity. Seven products. Every active process. Every complexity tier we could get into that run. The time estimates in the model are not theoretical. They are measured.

Formalization changes things. When you ask an expert to articulate their knowledge, they often realize they don't know it as precisely as they thought. The process of articulation creates new clarity — and reveals new uncertainty. This is uncomfortable. The expert feels exposed. The interviewer has to be careful not to undermine the trust that makes the extraction possible.
S.07 · Intercepting the Workflow

Don't Change Behavior. Intercept It.

The failure mode of most digitization projects: you ask people to do new things. We didn't.

When a production order arrives, Hernán draws a sketch. He's always drawn sketches. The sketch goes to each operator with annotations — dimensions, material specifications, process indications, special notes. It's his primary communication tool and it contains everything we need: dimensions, material, process flags, complexity indicators.

We didn't ask Hernán to learn software. We didn't ask him to fill forms. We asked him to keep doing exactly what he already does — draw his sketch — and we built the computer system around his native output. His friction is zero. The system's data is rich.

The production manager reviews and confirms each extraction. When the model is wrong, he corrects it. The correction teaches the model. Hernán becomes more consistent because he has to articulate the exception. This is a feedback loop between human expertise and machine structure.

S.08 · The Philosophy

Augmentation, Not Replacement

The easy version of this story is: "we used AI to price products." That misses the point entirely.

"The production manager is not being replaced. He is being equipped with a mirror — a system that reflects his own knowledge back to him in a form that can be shared, challenged, updated, and improved."

The AI (vision model, classification model) is not the innovation. The innovation is the epistemological work: making the implicit explicit, structuring what was intuitive, building a system that can hold and communicate expert knowledge consistently across time and people.

The most valuable application of AI in manufacturing is not automation of tasks. It's the formalization of expertise. The 30-year veteran who retires takes his knowledge with him. If you've built the system, the knowledge stays.

The honest caveat

This is hard. It takes longer than you expect. The expert has to trust you. The organization has to commit to maintaining the model. The data quality depends on human diligence. There is no version of this that runs itself.
