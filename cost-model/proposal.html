The Factory That Prices Products Out of Thin Air

A Chilean stainless steel manufacturer. Every product different. One pricing method for all of them.

Ingeniería en Aceros (Dulox) makes custom stainless steel products for restaurants, industrial kitchens, hotels, and construction sites. Every product is different — different dimensions, different processes, different complexity. For years, the pricing method has been: cost of materials × a factor. That factor absorbs all factory overhead spread uniformly across everything.

The Real Problem Is Decomposition

This isn't a pricing problem. It's a decomposition problem.

Before you can price something, you have to be able to decompose it — break it into its cost primitives. For a stainless steel product: materials, direct labor per process, machine time, consumables. The reason the factor has worked is that nobody had done this decomposition. It requires sitting with the production manager and mapping out what happens to a product from raw steel sheet to finished piece.

The real challenge: every product is custom. You need a model that generalizes — that can take any new product and estimate cost without having measured it before.

The structural insight

Classify products into profiles (what processes they go through) and complexity tiers (how hard each process is for this specific product). If you know both, you can compute cost. If you can generalize both to new products, you have a pricing engine.

What a Product Is Determines What It Costs

Step one: classify every SKU by its manufacturing identity — not its commercial category.

Two products that look nothing alike can share a process profile if they go through the same factory operations. A cylindrical trash bin and a custom pot both go through rolling, welding, and polishing — their profiles are identical. The grouping is by manufacturing DNA, not shape or appearance.

The Knowledge Problem

The hardest part of the project had nothing to do with data or models.

Hernán has worked at Dulox for over thirty years. He knows, intuitively, how long every process takes. He knows which products are going to be trouble before they're even started. This knowledge is real, accurate, and completely informal. It exists only in his head.

The first sessions were hard. He'd say "that takes about 40 minutes" and when you asked "for what size piece?" he'd think for a second and say "well, it depends." The job was to make the it depends explicit.

Formalization changes things. When you ask an expert to articulate their knowledge, they often realize they don't know it as precisely as they thought. The process of articulation creates new clarity — and reveals new uncertainty. The expert feels exposed.

Intercepting the Existing Workflow

Don't change the workflow. Find the existing artifact that already captures what you need.

When a production order arrives, Hernán draws a sketch. He's always drawn sketches. The sketch contains dimensions, material specs, process indications, complexity flags, special notes. Everything the model needs. We didn't ask him to learn software. We asked him to keep doing exactly what he already does — and built the system around his native output.

Section 08
08

Augmentation, Not Replacement

The easy version of this story is "we used AI to price products." That misses the point entirely.

Hernán is not being replaced. He is being equipped with a mirror — a system that reflects his own knowledge back to him in a form that can be shared, challenged, updated, and improved. When the model is wrong, Hernán corrects it. The correction teaches the model. Hernán becomes more consistent because he has to articulate the exception.

The deeper claim

The most valuable application of AI in manufacturing is not automation of tasks. It's the formalization of expertise. The 30-year veteran who retires takes his knowledge with him. If you've built the system, the knowledge stays.
