What machines can't hold

They'd been in business thirty years and couldn't tell you which products made money.

A manufacturing company in Chile priced everything the same way for three decades. It worked — until you looked closely. This is how a whiteboard sketch became a pricing engine, and why the real project was never really about pricing.

Act One
The problemA business blind
to its own costs

A business that doesn't know its own costs.

The company makes stainless steel products. Custom work — restaurant kitchens, trash bins, counters, range hoods, shelves. Everything is different. Different size, different shape, different amount of work. No two orders quite alike.

For thirty years, they priced everything the same way:

That number was supposed to cover all the rest of it — the operators, the machines, the time, the electricity, the consumables. One figure standing in for everything the formula couldn't see.

And honestly? It worked. The company was profitable. So nobody questioned it.

The problem only shows up when you look closer. That single number treats a plain flat shelf exactly the same as a cylindrical bin with interior welds and a mirror-polish finish. Those two products are not remotely the same amount of work.

the margin just quietly bleeds — and nobody connects the dots

This is how a structural problem hides inside a profitable business. The salesperson quotes a complex order too cheap, wins the deal, and the factory loses money making it. The only reason it hadn't become a crisis was volume and luck. That is not a plan.

p. 02
Act Two
The reframeCan't fix what
you can't see

You can't fix what you can't see.

The root cause wasn't the multiplier. The multiplier was a symptom. The real problem was that nobody had ever broken down what it actually costs to make a specific product — not in any systematic way.

Before you can price something correctly, you have to be able to take it apart. Turn it into its real cost pieces: the material, the time at each step, the wear on the machines, the consumables. Each part, each step, each hour. The factory had never done this — not out of negligence, but because it's genuinely hard. Every product is custom. There's no standard price list.

The real challenge was never the maths. It was the decomposition.

p. 03
Act Three
RoutesManufacturing
DNA

Every product speaks the same language, if you know how to listen.

Even though every product is custom, they don't all take a unique path through the factory. They share routes. A cylindrical bin and a custom bucket have different dimensions, but they both go through cutting, rolling, welding, and polishing. The route is the same. Only the difficulty changes.

Think of it as a product's manufacturing DNA. Two things that look nothing alike can share the same DNA if they pass through the same steps. We sorted all thirteen hundred products this way — not by what they look like, but by what happens to them on the floor.

Act Five
The hard partKnowledge in
one head

The knowledge lives inside one person's head.

Here's where the story gets harder. None of this model works without the data inside it — and that data isn't in a spreadsheet anywhere. It's in the head of the production manager. Call him Hernán. He's worked here more than thirty years.

He knows, by instinct, how long every step takes. He knows which products will be trouble before they even start. He can look at a sketch and feel the complexity. He's been doing it so long he doesn't think about it consciously anymore.

That knowledge is real. It is accurate. It is also completely invisible to everyone else in the building. Getting it out of his head was the hardest part of the whole project.

You can't just sit someone down and say tell me everything you know. That's not how expertise works. It's contextual. The early sessions were uncomfortable — he'd say that takes about forty minutes, and when you asked for what size? he'd stop and say well, it depends. The whole job was turning it depends into something concrete.

Every organisation has a Hernán.
Someone whose knowledge runs everything — without anyone realising.

And when that person leaves, they take all of it with them. The knowledge doesn't exist anywhere else. The organisation starts again from zero.

p. 06
Act Six
The methodIntercept the
workflow

Don't change the workflow. Intercept it.

Most “let's digitise the factory” projects fail the same way. They ask people to do new things — new software, new forms, new habits. These are people who've done their job for fifteen years without any of that. Every new step is friction. Friction becomes abandonment. The project dies.

The better move: don't change the workflow at all. Find the thing that already carries the information you need, and wrap the technology around that.

Act Seven
The resultWhen the number
is right

What happens when the number is right.

Salespeople stop guessing. They can quote a complex product with confidence — not because they've memorised every step, but because the system can show its work. This product takes this route, at this difficulty, and that's why it costs what it costs. A different conversation than the number says so.

The company can finally see margin by product type — for the first time in thirty years. Which categories are quietly underpriced. Which have room. They can stop letting simple work subsidise complex work without anyone deciding to.

New people don't need two years to absorb Hernán's instinct by osmosis. The model is a baseline that explains its own logic. Onboarding gets faster.

The factory didn't change. The machines are the same. Hernán still draws his sketches every morning — but now, when the sketch is done, the number is right.

p. 08
Closing
The bigger pointWhy this isn't
about pricing

This story is about one factory. But the pattern is everywhere. Every organisation has knowledge that lives inside specific people and nowhere else. That knowledge is exactly what makes them good at what they do. It is also a quiet fragility — because when those people leave, it leaves with them.

What's changed in the last few years is that we finally have tools to catch that knowledge in a way that wasn't practical before. Not to replace the expert. To give the expert a mirror — a way to see, share, and sharpen what they already know.

The AI part of this project is not the impressive part. The impressive part is the human work underneath: convincing an expert to say out loud what he'd never had to say, shaping it into something consistent, and building something that holds it over time.

Knowledge hides where nobody is looking.

Experts surface it every day without realising. The job is to catch it before it disappears — the pricing engine is just the output.
