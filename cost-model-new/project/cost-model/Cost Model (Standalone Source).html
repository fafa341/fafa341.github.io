really costs?

Every new order at this company starts as a sketch like the one above. Measurements and a language that only the operators know. That sketch tells the entire cost of a product — which materials, which processes it will go through and for how much time. If you know how to read it.

What follows is the working notebook: how we didn't introduce new technology to the way they operate — but how we taught the computer to read their language, how this reveals the true costs of the business, lets them know how to optimize, where to place their bets, and price competitively.

A story on how fixing structure can give you an edge and how that matters for a solid growth plan.

the premise

SECTION 01

Many businesses don't know their exact costs.

Companies that make custom work struggle with this. Custom kitchens, trash bins, counters, range hoods, shelves — everything is different. Sizes, shapes, amount of work. No two orders quite alike.

For years they price to cover costs with hard intuition. It makes them profitable. It works and nobody questions it. But it hides the ability to price competitively — knowing real margin per product, effort per product, optimization alternatives, and where to place their bets.

It is usually like this: Price = materials × a number. One multiplier meant to cover everything — operators, machines, time, electricity, consumables. One figure standing in for everything the formula cannot see.

SECTION 02

The root cause isn't the multiplier. It's that it's hard to break things down.

Breaking down what it actually costs to make a specific custom product is genuinely hard if you are not the one building it. There are no automatic robots, just humans. Repetition across multiple years is what builds that knowledge.

So you keep breaking it down. The real challenge isn't the math — it's the decomposition. Before you can price something correctly, you have to be able to take it apart. Turn it into its real cost pieces: material, time at each step, machine wear, consumables. Each part, each step, each hour.

Even though every product is custom, they don't all take a unique path through the factory. They share routes. A cylindrical bin and a custom bucket have different dimensions, but they both go through cutting, rolling, welding, and polishing. The route is the same. Only the difficulty changes.

A · The opaque price UNKNOWABLE

A monolithic number. Profitable in aggregate, illegible per product. No signal about which products earn, which leak, which are time-bombs in the queue.

B · Decomposed into primitives COMPUTABLE

Five primitives. Five clean inputs. A real number per product, traceable line by line. The decomposition is the engine.

Think of it as a product's manufacturing DNA. Two things that look nothing alike can share the same DNA if they pass through the same steps. We sorted all 1,300 products this way — not by what they look like, but by what happens to them on the floor.

SECTION 03

Every product speaks the same language, if you know how to listen.

Sort products not by what they look like — by what happens to them on the factory floor. That's the route. That's the DNA.

Step one: go through every one of the 1,300+ SKUs and assign it a process profile — the sequence of manufacturing operations it goes through. Two products that look nothing alike share a profile if they take the same route across the floor. A cylindrical bin and a custom bucket are siblings. A shelf and a counter panel are siblings. Commercial category means nothing here — what matters is the route.

design constraint

It would have been easy to make dozens of micro-categories. We resisted. The goal: the smallest number of profiles that still captures real cost variation. Too many and the model becomes unmaintainable. Too few and it loses resolution. This is not a product category in the commercial sense — it's a manufacturing identity.

tier mechanic

Each process uses a subset of the four drivers. Welding uses G, C and X. Polishing uses G and X. Bending uses G, D and C. Each combination gives a score that maps to a complexity tier — C1 (standard), C2 (elevated), C3 (expert). Each tier has calibrated time, operator count and consumables from real production measurements.

calibration note

You can't measure every product — there are 1,300 SKUs. So you calibrate on anchor products, the most common, covering the widest range of processes and complexity. Everything else extrapolates from the nearest anchor, dimensions scaling the time estimates proportionally.

SECTION 06

The knowledge lives inside one person's head.

Here the story gets harder. None of this model works without the data inside it — and that data isn't in a spreadsheet anywhere.

He knew, by instinct, how long every step takes. He knows which products will be trouble before they even start. He can look at a sketch and feel the complexity. He has been doing it so long he does not think about it consciously anymore.

That knowledge is real. It is accurate. And it is completely invisible to everyone else in the building. Getting it out of his head was the hardest part of the whole project.

You cannot just sit someone down and say tell me everything you know. That's not how expertise works. It's contextual. The early sessions were uncomfortable — he'd say that takes about forty minutes, and when you asked for what size? he'd stop and say well, it depends. The whole job was turning it depends into something concrete.

A · Articulation

Every organisation has a Hernán.

Someone whose knowledge runs everything without anyone realising. When that person leaves, they take all of it with them. The knowledge does not exist anywhere else. The organisation starts again from zero.

B · Trust

The whole project lived inside that pause.

You cannot rush it. The trust matters. The knowledge surfaces in weeks of contextual conversations, not a single interview. Every it depends is a piece of the model waiting to be made concrete.

You are not building a pricing tool. You are building a mirror — a system that reflects an expert's own knowledge back to him in a form that can be shared, challenged, updated, and improved. The machine learns from corrections. The expert sharpens their own thinking by having to explain the exception.

SECTION 07

Don't change the workflow. Intercept it.

Not the "let's digitise the factory" approach — that fails every time. New software, new forms, new habits nobody adopts. Friction becomes abandonment. What if we use what current technology actually can do?

This is a human-computer interaction problem. Find the thing that already carries the information you need, and wrap the technology around that. The expert already draws a sketch for every order. Operators add to it. It carries everything we need. We just need a simple loop that barely touches him at all.

design principle

We did not ask the expert to learn software. We did not ask him to fill forms. We asked him to keep doing exactly what he already does — draw his sketch — and built the system around his native output. His friction is almost zero. The system captures almost everything.

SECTION 08

The bigger point.

This story is about one factory. The pattern is everywhere. Every organisation has knowledge that lives inside specific people and nowhere else.

This pushes for change. Causes a market change. Experts still draw sketches every morning — but now when the sketch is done, everyone speaks the same economic language. You can intercept it with new tech and that is what we are pushing for. Maybe new AI tools for sketching, new automatic robotic machines, anything — but now all language is formalized and variations are tracked.

When those people leave, everything leaves with them. Each organisation speaks its own language. The work is to formalise that language — structure it so a process can be built around it, stored, and transmitted. A better way for an organisation to learn, theorize, and execute.

The most valuable application of AI here is not the automation of tasks. It is the formalization of expertise. The 30-year veteran who retires takes his knowledge with him. If you have built the system, the knowledge stays.

Salespeople stop guessing. They can quote complex products with confidence — the system shows its work. The company sees margin by product type for the first time in thirty years. Which categories are underpriced, which have room, where to place bets on complex work. Price sharper, hold the line where there is no room. Mix with imported parts, discriminate costs, deliver a great product. Then push hard on growth — when you can track everything.

New people do not need two years absorbing expert instinct by osmosis. The model is a baseline that explains its own logic. Build and quote complex projects in minutes.

The factory did not change. The machines are the same. The operators are the same. Hernán still draws his sketches every morning — but now, when the sketch is done, the number is right.
